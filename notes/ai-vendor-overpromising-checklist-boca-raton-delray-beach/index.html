Signals That an AI Vendor Is Overpromising: A Buyer’s Checklist for Boca Raton and Delray Beach Businesses

Ten red flags in the pitch, the contract and the credentials, and eight questions to bring to the meeting, from the team behind the Tokenmaxxing essay.

By Jason Colapietro, Founder and CEO, Suede AI · West Palm Beach, FL · November 15, 2026

Picture a vendor who walks into a law office off Palmetto Park Road in Boca Raton with a demo that drafts a client letter in eight seconds. The partners are impressed, the contract runs twelve months, and nobody asks what happens when the demo meets the firm’s real files. That gap between the demo and the deployment is where most AI overpromising lives.

Jason Colapietro wrote the Tokenmaxxing essay about spending that nobody can connect to value, at the scale of a reported $500 million monthly AI bill. Overpromising is the sales-side twin of that problem: a vendor who promises more than the technology delivers is setting you up for an invoice you cannot explain later. Jason Colapietro and the Suede team wrote this checklist for buyers who want a second opinion on an AI proposal before they sign. It is written for owners and office managers in Boca Raton and Delray Beach, where law firms, wealth managers, medical practices and the restaurants along Atlantic Avenue all draw a steady stream of AI pitches, but it works anywhere.

Red flags in the pitch itself

1. A guaranteed result in search or AI answers. No one controls what Google, ChatGPT or Perplexity says. A vendor can improve the odds that your business is cited, and can measure it, but “page one in 30 days” or “ChatGPT will recommend you” is a sales line. Ask how they will measure citations before and after, and what they do if the number does not move.

2. Accuracy numbers with no test set. “98% accurate” means nothing until you know accurate on what. Ask for the evaluation: how many sample tasks, drawn from where, graded by whom. A Delray Beach property manager should want an agent tested on real lease questions, not on the vendor’s tidy examples. Our sister site Agentix walks through how to grade an AI agent before you deploy it, and it is a fair standard to hold any vendor to.

3. “Fully autonomous” for work that carries liability. A medical intake form, a filing deadline or a trust account reconciliation needs a human approval step. A vendor who sells the removal of that step as a feature is selling you risk.

4. A demo that never touches your data. The fastest test of a pitch is to hand over ten real, anonymized examples and watch. Jason Colapietro suggests calling it a ten-file test. A vendor who declines it is telling you how it would go.

Red flags in the contract

5. Usage pricing with no ceiling. If the plan bills per token, per message or per task, the contract should name a monthly cap and who is allowed to raise it. Without one, you have recreated the tokenmaxxing invoice at small-business scale.

6. Long terms for unproven work. A twelve-month commitment for a tool you have never run in production protects the vendor. Ask for a 90-day pilot with a written exit.

7. No export clause. Prompts, configurations, knowledge bases and conversation logs should leave with you in a standard format. If the vendor wraps another company’s model and goes under, your workflows should survive it.

8. Vague data handling. Ask where client data is processed, whether it trains anyone’s model, and how long logs are kept. For a Boca Raton wealth advisor or a Delray Beach dental office, “we take security seriously” is not an answer.

Red flags in the credentials

9. Borrowed or inflated affiliations. Startup programs, marketplace listings and course badges are real and useful, but they are not partnerships. If a vendor claims a partnership with a major AI lab, ask for the program name and a link. Here is how we state ours, so you have a template: Suede AI is a member of Claude for Startups, Anthropic’s startup program, and of Google Cloud Partner Advantage through JC Investment Group LLC. Neither membership is an endorsement of our client work, and we do not describe either one that way.

10. Rankings with no date, category or geography. A badge that says “Top AI Agency” with no source is decoration. A real ranking names the publisher, the date, the category and the region. What a credible vendor shows instead

Overpromising is easiest to spot next to its opposite, which is public work you can check. Jason Colapietro, who founded Suede AI in West Palm Beach in 2024, points buyers to records that do not depend on his word: 54 pull requests merged across 46 open-source repositories he does not maintain, including Jest, Adobe React Spectrum, Backstage and the Polkadot SDK, each reviewed by a maintainer with no reason to be generous. He has shipped 9 iOS apps under his own Apple developer account and written five books on Amazon, among them The Claude Code Bible and Codex in Production. Every vendor does not need that record. Every claim on a vendor’s slide should link somewhere, though, the way each line on Suede’s does.

A credible vendor also measures before selling. If your website is the first thing an AI assistant reads about your business, an objective baseline is worth more than any promise. The Suede Scan notes explain what an AI readiness score measures, and that kind of baseline is the right first deliverable from any vendor: a number you can check again in 90 days.

Eight questions to bring to the meeting

  • What single outcome will this move, and how will we measure it?
  • Will you run your system on ten of our real examples before we sign?
  • What is the monthly spending cap, and who can change it?
  • Is there a 90-day pilot with a written exit?
  • What do we keep, in what format, if we leave or you close?
  • Where is our data processed, and does it train any model?
  • Which step keeps a human in the loop?
  • Can you link to proof for every credential you just showed us?

A vendor who answers all eight cleanly is worth a pilot. A vendor who gets defensive at the second question has answered the rest.
