Measuring AI ROI in a Small Business: A 90-Day Scorecard

Five lines on one page, a baseline week, three check-ins and a decision rule: how Suede AI tells a working AI project from a meter that just runs.

By Jason Colapietro, Founder and CEO, Suede AI · West Palm Beach, FL · November 15, 2026

Ninety days is long enough for an AI project to show a result and short enough that a failed one costs a quarter instead of a year. Most small businesses never learn which kind they bought, because nobody wrote down what “working” meant before the tool went live.

The Tokenmaxxing essay by Jason Colapietro closes on Uber’s COO admitting he could not yet draw the line from the company’s AI spend to the product. That is a candid admission at a company with thousands of engineers. A twelve-person insurance agency in West Palm Beach or a landscaping company in Palm Beach Gardens cannot carry the same uncertainty for long. This scorecard is the one-page method Suede AI recommends for drawing that line in a small business. Suede AI (formerly Suede Labs) wrote it for owners who want a plain answer by day 90.

Week zero: measure before anything goes live

ROI needs a before. Spend the first week recording the current state of the one workflow you plan to change, by hand if necessary. If the project is an after-hours intake agent, count how many inquiries arrive outside business hours, how many get a reply by 9 a.m., and how many become booked appointments. If the project is AI search visibility, record which of ten realistic customer questions currently name your business in ChatGPT, Gemini, Perplexity and Google’s AI results.

Jason Colapietro is strict about this step because it is the one most often skipped. Without a baseline, any number at month three looks like progress.

The five lines on the scorecard

The Suede scorecard uses the same five lines, whatever the project:

  1. Outcome metric. The one number the project exists to move: booked calls, quotes sent, hours of admin removed, citations in AI answers. Chosen in week zero and never swapped mid-quarter.
  2. Time recovered. Staff hours per week no longer spent on the task, multiplied by a loaded hourly cost. Count only time that was actually reassigned to other work.
  3. Total cost. Setup fees, subscriptions, model usage and the staff time spent supervising the system. Supervision is the cost most often left out.
  4. Quality rate. The share of outputs a person accepted without edits, sampled weekly. A drafting tool that saves an hour but needs forty minutes of corrections saved twenty minutes.
  5. Risk events. Wrong answers sent to customers, data handled outside policy, spending-cap breaches. The target is zero, and each one gets a written note.

Turning the lines into one ROI figure

The arithmetic is simple on purpose. Add the value of the outcome change (extra booked jobs times average gross margin, for example) to the value of time recovered, subtract total cost, and divide by total cost.

A worked example with round, illustrative numbers: an intake agent costs $600 a month all-in, including two hours a week of review. It recovers six staff hours a week at $35 an hour, about $900 a month, and adds four booked jobs a month at $250 of margin each, another $1,000. That is $1,900 of value on $600 of cost, a monthly ROI of roughly 217%. If the bookings line had stayed flat, the same project would return 50% on time savings alone. Still positive, but a different decision about whether to expand it.

Two rules keep the figure honest. Count only changes that show up against the week-zero baseline. And never count model usage as a benefit. Tokens consumed are a cost line, which is the whole argument of tokenmaxxing.

Days 30, 60 and 90: what each check-in decides

Day 30: is it running safely? The outcome metric is usually noisy this early. The questions are whether the quality rate is above 80%, whether any risk events occurred, and whether spending stayed under the cap. Fix or stop here; never wait until day 90 to deal with a safety problem.

Day 60: is the outcome moving? Compare the outcome metric to the baseline. A flat line at day 60 does not end the project, but it should trigger one specific change, such as a narrower scope, a better prompt or a different handoff point, rather than a vague plan to keep watching.

Day 90: expand, adjust or cut. If ROI is positive and the quality rate held, widen the scope or add a second workflow. If ROI is near zero but the outcome is trending up, run one more 30-day cycle with a written target. If ROI is negative, cut the project and keep the lessons. Cutting is the method working, not the business failing. Jason Colapietro’s point is that the rule only works if it also applies to the projects you are most excited about.

The best first projects are narrow, frequent and already measured somewhere, such as a phone log, a CRM stage or a calendar.
